Framing complex problems: a way of doing fixed charge receiverships

Expansion, abstraction, abduction, exaptation, variation, probing and reflexivity, the disruption of entrainment, and the Fantastic, set to work on the charged asset in distress

A receiver is handed a problem that has already been framed. The deed of appointment names the security, the lender names the debt, and the standard instruction names the route: secure, market, sell, account. This treatise argues that in complex appointments the most valuable thing a receiver does comes before any of that. It is how the receiver frames the situation. Frames emerge from configurations of relationships, actors, practices, meanings, material conditions and constraints, and they shape those configurations in return. Change the frame and the conditions for action change too. Change the conditions and a different way of seeing becomes possible.

01 · The field

The appointment letter is already a frame

A fixed charge receivership begins with a document that presents itself as neutral. The deed of appointment names the property, recites the power, and hands the receiver a short list of verbs: take possession of the income, collect, manage, insure, repair, let, sell, account. Before anyone has asked a question, those verbs already hold an answer. The problem, the instruction implies, is a defaulted loan. The solution is a sale. The receiver is the instrument that turns one into the other.

Anyone who has done this work for long enough knows the instruction is not wrong. It is incomplete in a way that matters. The defaulted loan is only where a much larger pattern became visible to a credit department. Behind it stand a building with a history, tenants with businesses of their own, a borrower with a family and a story, a town whose centre of gravity has moved, a planning regime, a rates bill, an insurance policy with a vacancy clause, a roof that leaks in January, and a market that has priced in everything except what could be done here. The loan is the symptom that comes with a number.

This treatise sets out a way of doing receiverships that starts from that recognition. It takes the practice of reframing complex problems developed by Professor Kees Dorst, Rodger Watson and colleagues over many years: seven practices (expansion, abstraction, abduction, exaptation, variation, probing and reflexivity) gathered around the disruption of entrainment. It puts that practice to work in the receiver’s world. Alongside it runs Ben Zweibelson’s model of reflective practice. Zweibelson begins innovation in what he calls the Fantastic, which he describes as the infinity of human abstraction, and insists that deliberate action is always answered by consequences in a fluid reality.

A receivership is not a transaction. It is an intervention in a configuration.

The configuration produced the default. The configuration will produce the recovery, or fail to. How the receiver frames the configuration in the first fortnight shapes what value can be recovered, and what harm can be avoided, more than any later decision.

Four kinds of appointment

Not every appointment needs reframing. A well-let modern unit with a strong tenant, a clean title and a deep buyer pool is a clear problem: the standard route is the right one, and the receiver who agonises over frames is wasting the lender’s money. The practice described here earns its keep in the other three kinds: appointments that are complicated (many parts, but knowable with expertise), complex (parts that act on each other, so the whole cannot be known in advance) and wicked (complex, contested and value-laden, where every attempted solution changes the problem). The first skill is telling them apart.

02 · Where frames come from

Frames emerge from configurations

Reframing complex problems is a practice of inquiry into changing compositions of relationships, actors, practices, meanings, material conditions and constraints, through which problems emerge and persist. These configurations go under many names: systems, assemblages, meshwork, matrix. The name matters less than one recognition. Frames are not only in people’s heads. They emerge from the configuration, the way a river lays down its own banks.

A frame is shaped by how people perceive, relate and act, and it shapes those things in return. It is also shaped by the material, cultural and historical conditions those people live in. The lender’s workout team sees the asset through the credit file. The borrower sees twenty years of ownership and a personal guarantee. The tenant sees a shopfront, a lease and a landlord who has stopped answering. The local authority sees a rates hereditament, a planning history and a parade it would like to see regenerated. Each frame is reasonable from where it is held. None of them, alone, is the problem.

Hard constraints and soft constraints

Hard constraints are the edges a receiver cannot cross. Soft constraints only feel like edges. A great deal of value in receivership is lost not to the market but to soft constraints mistaken for hard ones: “we always sell as one lot”, “the lender won’t fund works”, “flats above shops never sell”, “planning won’t wear it”. Each of these is sometimes true. None of them is true because it has always been said. Sort the statements below. Each one has been heard in a receivership meeting.

Treating habit as law is the most expensive error in receivership.

03 · Co-emergence

Ways of seeing, conditions for action

At the top of Dorst and Watson’s diagram two arrows chase each other around two phrases, ways of seeing and conditions for action, and between them sit two words: reciprocal, co-emergent. That is the heart of the practice. Reframing is not a change of opinion followed by a change of plan. It is a reciprocal transformation of both ways of seeing and the conditions for action: a co-emergent process in which our understanding of a situation and the situation itself develop together.

In receivership this is very concrete. The moment the receiver sees a void ground-floor unit as a possible meanwhile use rather than a rates liability, the conditions for action change. A licence is drafted, a local maker is found, the lights come on, the shopfront stops attracting graffiti, the neighbouring tenant renews, and a bidder walking the parade sees a living street instead of a dying one. The changed situation supports a different way of seeing, which supports a different action, which changes the situation again. Step through it below.

Turn of the loop

Dorst’s reasoning patterns

Dorst’s account of design reasoning gives the receiver a precise way of noticing which kind of problem is on the desk. He sets out reasoning as an equation: a what (a thing, a service, an arrangement) plus a how (a working principle) leads to a value. Choose a mode, or tap the boxes to cycle through them.

Mode
+→

Most receivership is done in the first three modes. The value is known: the best price reasonably obtainable, a timely and defensible recovery. Very often the how is known too: secure, stabilise, market, sell. Normal abduction then asks only what to sell, and in which lots. Complex receiverships are different. The value is still known, but neither the what nor the how is given. That is design abduction, and it cannot be solved head-on. Dorst’s answer is the frame: a proposed working principle that, if adopted, makes a what thinkable.

04 · Frame creation

Frame creation in a receiver’s hand

Dorst’s Frame Innovation (2015) sets out a sequence for creating frames that grew out of design practice and the Designing Out Crime work at the University of Technology Sydney, where Rodger Watson has long been a leading figure. It is not a recipe, and the seven practices in section 06 run through every step. But as a checklist for the first month of a complex appointment it is hard to better. Step through it.

The shape of a frame

A frame has a simple grammar. If we look at this situation as if it were X, and adopt the working principle that goes with X, then we will create the value we need. The metaphor is not decoration. It carries a working principle from somewhere it already works. The best-known illustration in Dorst’s work concerns Sydney’s Kings Cross entertainment district, where problems of late-night disorder were approached not only as a policing matter but through the frame of a well-run music festival: a large crowd of people who want a good night out and need the infrastructure, flows and care that festivals provide. The working principle travelled, and the solutions followed from it.

The receiver learns to make the same move. Build a frame below. Choose a situation and a metaphor, and see the working principle it brings, what it would change, what it would cost, and the test that would tell you whether it holds.

05 · Entrainment

When the pattern plays itself

At the centre of Dorst and Watson’s diagram sits a hand-drawn network of filled and hollow nodes and crossing curves, and in one corner a coil of lines wound tight on itself. That coil is entrainment: dominant patterns of organisation that become mutually reinforcing and taken for granted. Pendulum clocks hung on one wall fall into step. So do professions.

Receivership is unusually prone to entrainment because it is repeated, regulated and paid for by a small number of repeat instructors. The lender’s panel terms, the receiver’s standard report, the valuer’s comparables, the selling agent’s auction catalogue, the solicitor’s precedent sale contract and the credit committee’s appetite for certainty all reinforce each other. The loop is efficient, defensible and fast. It is also blind. It sees what it was built to see.

Where receivership entrainment comes from

Entrainment is not a moral failing. It is what competent people produce when they repeat good-enough work under pressure. Its sources in receivership are easy to name: panel agreements that specify a reporting format and therefore a way of thinking; fee bases that reward speed or realisations; key performance indicators measured in days to sale; a precedent bank that makes the last answer the cheapest next answer; a vocabulary of exit; and the very real anxiety of a credit committee that has to explain a loss. None of these is wrong. Together they make alternatives literally invisible.

06 · The seven practices

Seven practices around entrainment

Dorst and Watson’s diagram arranges seven practices around the entrained configuration: expansion, abstraction and abduction on the left; exaptation, variation and probing on the right; reflexivity beneath, its eye turned back on the observer. Through them we intentionally explore different arrangements, creating new environments and contexts in which qualitatively different forms of organisation and understanding can emerge. They are not linear and they are not independent. Together they are a practice of engaging with, and changing, the conditions of complex situations.

One movement, not seven steps

Read in sequence, the seven practices look like a method. In use they are more like a dance. Expansion widens the field and so gives abstraction more to work with. Abstraction reveals a pattern that invites an abductive leap. The leap finds its means through exaptation from another field. Variation multiplies the candidate frames. Probing tests them against the situation’s own responses. Reflexivity asks, at every turn, what the receiver’s own position is doing to what can be seen. Then the result of the probe widens the field again. That is why the practice cannot be reduced to a checklist on day one and a sale on day ninety. The configuration answers back.

07 · Expansion

Broaden the field

Expansion broadens the field of inquiry to include overlooked relationships, indirect actors, wider contexts and interconnected systems that shape and sustain the problem. In receivership the habitual field is the charged title, the rent roll and the comparables. The real field is wider. It includes the anchor that closed across the road, the bus route that moved, the council’s town-centre plan, the borrower’s related companies and other lenders, the neighbouring owner with a ransom strip, the residential occupiers upstairs, and the people who walk past at eleven at night.

Dorst’s steps three and four make the move precise: first the context, the inner circle of players with a direct stake; then the field, the wider ring of people and organisations who could be part of a solution even though they are not yet part of the problem. The map below shows both rings for a mixed-use parade. Switch rings on and off, and tap an actor to see what the habitual frame misses.

Rings

The archaeology of the asset

Dorst’s first step is archaeology: how did this situation come to be? In receivership that means reading the title and its plans, the planning history, the lease files and their variations, the credit file and its covenants, the managing agent’s correspondence, the insurance claims history, and the borrower’s own account. It also means asking the building. A dated extension, a blocked rear door, a sub-meter, a ghost sign on a gable end: each is evidence of an earlier configuration and a clue to the next one. Archaeology is the cheapest expansion there is, and the most often skipped.

Expansion without scope creep

Expansion is inquiry, not activity. The receiver widens what is looked at, not what is done. Every widening is recorded, briefly, with the reason. If a wider look shows nothing, the record shows the receiver looked. If it shows something, the record shows why the strategy changed.

08 · Abstraction

Move between levels

Abstraction means moving between levels of understanding to reveal the underlying patterns, values, relationships and organising principles beyond the immediate problem. The immediate problem in a receivership is usually stated at one level: a unit is vacant, a tenant is in arrears, the bid is short of the debt. The pattern that produces it usually sits at another level. A ladder helps. Climb it from the component to the credit cycle, and notice what becomes visible at each rung.

Themes beneath positions

Abstraction also works on people. Positions are what parties say they want: “we want the arrears paid”, “we want more time”, “we want the flat repaired”, “we want our money”. Themes are the deeper needs that recur beneath them: certainty, dignity, continuity, safety, fairness, a functioning street. Dorst’s fifth step, themes, asks the practitioner to find them, because frames are built from themes, not from positions. A lender’s demand for “a sale in twelve weeks” is often a theme of certainty wearing a deadline. Offer certainty in another form (an agreed staged plan with dated decision points and stop rules) and the deadline may relax.

Positions are where people stand. Themes are why they stand there.

09 · Abduction

New explanations

Abduction generates plausible new explanations and possibilities that challenge existing assumptions and open alternative ways of understanding. Charles Sanders Peirce named it as the logic of the hypothesis: the surprising fact is observed; if this were so, the fact would be a matter of course; so there is reason to suspect that this is so. Zweibelson stresses that abduction involves improvisation, and that it is how hypotheses get formed in the incomplete, emergent conditions practitioners actually face.

In receivership the most important abductive question is also the most neglected: why did this default really happen? The credit file gives one answer, usually leverage or arrears. But the explanation chosen decides the route. Tick the clues found in the Station Road file (section 16). The bars show how strongly the evidence points to each explanation, and the leading explanation shows the route it opens. Notice how often more than one explanation stays alive.

Plausibility of each explanation

Hunch and flux

Drawing on Karl Weick, Zweibelson describes early ideas as hunches that take shape and dissolve in flux. A receiver should hold hunches in exactly that way. Write them down early, give each a test, and let most of them go. The danger is not having a wrong hunch. It is having one hunch and never noticing it is a hunch. The commonest unexamined hunch in receivership is “this is a sale”.

10 · Exaptation

Repurpose from elsewhere

Exaptation translates and repurposes ideas, capabilities, resources and practices from other, often seemingly unrelated, contexts. The term comes from evolutionary biology, where Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba used it in 1982 for features that evolved for one purpose and were co-opted for another: feathers that kept dinosaurs warm before they helped birds fly. In organisations it names the most underused source of novelty there is. Somewhere, in another field, someone has already solved a version of this problem in another key.

Receivership is a good borrower because it touches so many fields: property, insolvency, law, housing, hospitality, regeneration, construction, security, finance. 11 · Variation

Diverse perspectives

Variation introduces diverse and novel perspectives, conditions or probes. W. Ross Ashby’s law of requisite variety gives the reason: only variety can absorb variety. A complex appointment throws up more kinds of problem than any one discipline can see. A receiver who reads it only as a surveyor will see what surveyors see, and a narrow panel of perspectives produces a narrow range of prices.

Build an advisory bench for the Station Road parade (section 16). Each perspective lights up blind spots it would catch. Watch how few are lit by the default bench of valuer, agent and solicitor.

Bench

Variation in valuation and route

Variation applies to methods as well as people. One valuation basis tells you one thing. Ask for several: as is, with vacant possession of the voids, as let after stabilisation, in separate lots, for an alternative use with planning, as a going concern where there is a business. Likewise with routes: auction, private treaty, informal tender, off-market approach to special purchasers, sale to sitting tenants, a portfolio sale. Running more than one, where the duty and the costs allow, is not indecision. It is part of taking reasonable care to obtain the best price, and it is the receiver’s best defence when a price is later challenged.

12 · Probing

Situated interventions

Probing means making situated interventions to discover how a system responds and what becomes possible. In a complex appointment the receiver cannot know in advance how the tenants, the street, the buyer pool or the council will respond. The only way to find out is to act a little, watch carefully and decide again. A good probe is small, safe to fail, quick to read, and reversible. It has a cost, a time limit, a success signal, a failure signal and a stop rule, all agreed with the appointor before it starts.

Build a probe portfolio below. The meters show what it costs, how long it runs, how much it would teach, and how much of the downside is reversible. A good portfolio learns a lot cheaply and keeps the downside small.

Amplify and dampen

A probe is only useful if someone reads it. Decide in advance what would count as a signal. Then, when a probe works, amplify it: a second meanwhile licence, a wider marketing test, a fuller planning application. When a probe fails, dampen it: end the licence, record what was learned, move on. Medforth v Blake is the reminder that once a receiver chooses to manage, he must manage with due diligence. Probes are run properly, insured, documented and reported, or they are not run at all.

13 · Reflexivity

Examine ourselves

Reflexivity means critically examining how our own assumptions, values and actions shape the problems we perceive and the situations we seek to transform. It sits at the bottom of Dorst and Watson’s diagram as an eye with an arrow curling back on itself. It is the practice most often skipped, because it turns the inquiry on the inquirer.

Run the mirror on your current appointment. It does not produce a score. It shows which lenses are most likely to be shaping what you can see.

14 · The Fantastic

The Fantastic, and the fluid reality that answers it

Ben Zweibelson, writing in Contemporary Issues in Air and Space Power in March 2025, closes a four-part series on why militaries stifle new ideas with a model for reflective practice. His target is the Newtonian, problem-and-solution habit of institutions that move by increments within an inherited paradigm. His prescription is to begin innovation where institutions rarely allow it to begin: in the Fantastic, abstraction beyond institutional and individual frame limits, where imagination may run even to the seemingly absurd, disturbing or impossible.

Receivership is not war, and the receiver is not a general. But the institutional pattern Zweibelson describes will be familiar to anyone who has sat in a lender’s workout meeting: a strong preference for the known route, a vocabulary of objectives and milestones, a suspicion of anything that sounds like speculation, and an organisational memory that rewards having followed the process more than having found the value.

A model for reflective practice. Ben Zweibelson, “Innovation Management: Embracing the Fantastic to Get the Military Moving in New Directions”, Contemporary Issues in Air and Space Power 3(1), 2025 (CC BY 4.0), reproduced there from Zweibelson (2023a).

The model moves through four zones: the Fantastic, Refined Ideation, Decision and Deliberate Action, and Consequences in Fluid Reality. Two great arrows circle beneath and above. Paradigm convergence pulls ideas back toward the legacy frame through critical reflection and retention. Paradigm divergence pushes outward through disruptive innovation. Zweibelson treats the two as intertwined rather than opposed, and he treats failure as integral to innovation rather than its opposite. Tap a zone or an arrow.

Cycle

The reversal: from concrete legacy to emergent abstraction

Zweibelson’s second figure shows a movement many institutions find disorienting. The habitual sequence runs from abstract doctrine down to concrete action. The innovator reverses it, starting in the concrete legacy (what the institution already does) and moving deliberately into greater abstraction, where new possibilities become thinkable. In receivership the concrete legacy is the standard instruction; the abstraction is the question of what this asset, this debt and this place could become. Tap each level.

Sequence

Hold both arrows

Zweibelson’s two arrows map almost exactly onto the receiver’s two loyalties. Convergence is the lender’s credit policy, the panel agreement, the standard report and the precedent: retention, consistency, defensibility. It is not the enemy; it protects everyone from expensive enthusiasms. Divergence is the receiver’s professional judgement that this asset is not like the last one, and the willingness to say so in writing, with evidence. Move the balance to see what each extreme produces.

18 · The law of the frame

Reframing is permission, not obligation

A practice of reframing that ignored the law would not be a practice for receivers; it would be a liability. In England and Wales the receiver’s position is shaped by the charge itself, by the Law of Property Act 1925 and by a well-known line of authority on the duties owed in equity. These are hard constraints. Read properly, they are also the frame within which reframing is permitted, and protected.

SourceWhat it establishes (in outline)What it means for reframing
The charge and LPA 1925 (ss.101, 109)The receiver’s powers come from the mortgage deed and statute. The receiver is deemed the agent of the mortgagor, not the lender.Read the deed first. Powers to let, manage, carry out works or apply for planning define the space of possible frames.
Cuckmere Brick v Mutual Finance [1971]A mortgagee exercising a power of sale must take reasonable care to obtain the true market value at the time of sale. The same standard is applied to receivers who sell.Variation in route, lotting and buyer pool is part of discharging the duty, not a departure from it.
Downsview Nominees v First City Corp [1993] (PC)The receiver’s primary duty is to the appointor. Duties to the mortgagor arise in equity and centre on good faith.Reframing must serve recovery. A frame that helps everyone except the appointor is the wrong frame.
Medforth v Blake [2000]Where a receiver chooses to manage, he must do so with due diligence and in good faith; the duties go beyond merely avoiding bad faith.Once a management frame is adopted (trading, letting, works) it must be run competently. Probes must be properly run probes.
Silven Properties v RBS [2003]A mortgagee or receiver is not obliged to improve the property or pursue planning before sale, and may choose the time of sale, subject to the duty of care on sale.Reframing is permission, not obligation. The receiver may pursue value-adding frames, but must not be paralysed by every one of them.
Not legal advice. This is a summary of principles for practitioners. Each appointment turns on its own deed and facts, and on the advice of the appointor’s and receiver’s solicitors.

Three disciplines

Read together, the authorities give the receiver a clear posture: free to reframe, but not bound to; in good faith throughout; competent in management if he manages at all; careful on sale. Reframing within that posture has three disciplines. Record the frames considered. A short note of the frames tried, the evidence and the reason for the chosen route is the best protection a receiver has against a borrower’s later challenge and a lender’s later doubt. Price the probe. Every intervention has a cost, a time and a downside; state them, and the result that would stop it, before running it. Keep the appointor in the loop. Divergence is defensible when the lender saw it coming, understood why, and agreed the stop rule.

20 · The receiver who frames

The eye at the bottom of the wheel

A receiver is not a neutral instrument. He arrives with a profession (usually surveying, sometimes insolvency or law), a firm with a fee model, a panel relationship that brings repeat work, an appetite for risk shaped by past claims, and a habit of seeing buildings through comparables. Each of these is a lens. Each brings some things into focus and blurs others.

Donald Schön called the professional’s capacity to notice, in the middle of practice, that the situation is talking back reflection-in-action. Zweibelson draws the line between reflective practitioners, who make sense of consequences and let them change the frame, and non-reflective ones, who loop back into convergence and repeat. The receiver who frames well keeps both loops in sight and knows which one he is in.

The spine: 道 · 理 · 勢

The practice sits naturally on the spine that runs through this imprint. 道 Dào is the way the situation is already moving: the slow drift of footfall, the credit cycle, the town’s centre of gravity. 理 Lǐ is the grain of things: the title, the lease, the fabric, the duty, the use class, the patterns that cannot be argued with. 勢 Shì is the potential stored in the configuration, the propensity of things that François Jullien taught Western readers to see and that Zweibelson cites among his sources. Reframing, in this light, is the art of reading 勢 before it is spent: finding where a small, well-placed intervention will let the configuration carry the recovery, rather than forcing a sale against the grain.

Read the shì before it is spent.

道 Dào · the way

  • Which way is footfall, demand and credit already moving?
  • What is the town becoming, whatever we do?
  • Where is this asset in its own life cycle?

理 Lǐ · the grain

  • What does the deed allow?
  • What do the leases, the title and the use class fix?
  • What will the fabric bear?

勢 Shì · the propensity

  • Where is potential stored, waiting for a small push?
  • Which probe would release it?
  • When will it be spent if nobody acts?
21 · Integration

A practice, not a project

Dorst’s ninth step is integration: folding what was learned back into the organisations involved, so the next situation starts from a better place. In receivership there are three places to fold it: the receiver’s firm, the lender’s panel, and the profession.

I

In the firm

A frame record annexed to every complex strategy report. A monthly “frame review” in which one live case is presented with its frames and probes. A shared borrowing board of exaptations that worked. Junior surveyors asked for their naïve reading before seniors speak.

II

With the lender

A complexity screen at instruction: clear, complicated, complex or wicked. For complex cases, a four-week reading period agreed up front. Staged strategy reports with options and stop rules. A short post-case note on frames and outcomes, so the panel learns too.

III

In the profession

Case studies of reframed recoveries, anonymised, shared through professional bodies. Training that teaches the seven practices alongside valuation and law. A vocabulary (entrainment, frame, probe, stop rule) that lets receivers explain divergence to credit committees.

IV

In the receiver

A personal log of hunches and how they resolved. One unfamiliar field read each quarter for exaptations. An honest annual look at which frames one reaches for first, and why.

The first thirty, sixty and ninety days

For a firm adopting the practice: in the first thirty days, add the complexity screen and the frame record to the house templates. By sixty, run the first frame reviews on two live cases and present one to a lender. By ninety, agree a reading period and stop-rule format with at least one panel, and publish an anonymised case note. None of this costs much. All of it changes what the next appointment can see.
